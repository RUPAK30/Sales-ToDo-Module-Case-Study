How a deal moves, and why it matters 3 steps · public benchmarks
STEP 1 · FIELD REP (RAHUL / NEHA) Act on the top 10 & ask for a discount

Out of 100+ leads, Rahul sees only the 10 most urgent tasks. If a shop wants a discount above a live scheme, he asks here—and his task freezes until a manager decides.

STEP 2 · MANAGER / HEAD (KAVITA / ARJUN) Approve within limits (or escalate in 24h)

Kavita sees the ask, the margin floor, and the dealer's overdue credit. She approves up to 10%. Anything bigger—or left undecided for 1 day—automatically goes to Arjun (Sales Head).

STEP 3 · FINANCE HANDOFF (EVERY RUPEE) Sealed with SHA-256 & checked at payment

Every approval gets a tamper-evident SHA-256 fingerprint and goes to Finance. If a shop short-pays a bill without approval, Finance sends it back here and blocks the rep's next discount.

Why I built it this way 6 decisions
  1. Ten, not everything. A list a rep can finish beats a list they ignore; the rest is counted, never hidden.
  2. Value only breaks ties. Ranking by deal size teaches cherry-picking; lateness and money-blocking stage come first.
  3. Route by limit, not by hierarchy. Kavita decides up to 10% within the margin floor; anything else goes to the head, and a day's silence escalates.
  4. Buckets, like a bank MIS. A task moves freely inside its bucket; a wrong-bucket move needs a reason and a second manager.
  5. Tamper-evident, not blockchain. One company keeps this record, so a hash chain with a daily anchor shared outside is enough. It's the model Microsoft's SQL Ledger uses, and it answers the auditor's Rule 11(g) question.
  6. Validate before building more. Next: shadow 3 field reps and 1 approver for a week to test the 10-cap and the 1-day escalation.

What I'd do differently: I fixed the cap at 10 from published research before watching a rep's real day. A pilot tests that first, and the cap becomes a setting if 10 is wrong.
